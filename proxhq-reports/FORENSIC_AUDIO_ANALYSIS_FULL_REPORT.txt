  SECTION 3 — PHASE 1: BASELINE SPECTRAL ANALYSIS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  WHAT THIS PHASE DID:
  Both tracks were analyzed in their original, unmodified form using seven
  independent acoustic measurements. Each measurement captures a different
  dimension of the audio.

  HOW EACH METRIC WORKS:
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  [1] MFCC — Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients
      Tool: librosa.feature.mfcc(y=audio, sr=sr, n_mfcc=20)
      What it measures: The "timbral fingerprint" — the texture and
      character of the sound. MFCCs capture how the energy is distributed
      across frequency bands in a way that models how human ears perceive
      timbre. Two tracks with the same MFCC profile sound like they were
      made with the same instruments, synthesizers, and production style.
      Similarity method: Cosine similarity between mean MFCC vectors.

  [2] CHROMA / CENS — Chromagram
      Tool: librosa.feature.chroma_cens(y=audio, sr=sr)
      What it measures: The harmonic content — which musical notes and
      chords are present. Chroma maps all energy onto 12 pitch classes
      (C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B) regardless of octave.
      Similarity method: Cosine similarity tested at all 12 transpositions
      (shift 0–11 semitones) to find the best harmonic alignment.

  [3] TONNETZ — Tonal Centroid Space
      Tool: librosa.feature.tonnetz(y=harmonic_signal, sr=sr)
      What it measures: Harmonic relationships in 6-dimensional space,
      capturing how chords and notes relate to each other (fifths, thirds,
      minor thirds). More sensitive to harmonic movement than raw chroma.
      Similarity method: Cosine similarity between mean Tonnetz vectors.

  [4] IOI — Inter-Onset Interval Pattern
      Tool: librosa.beat.beat_track() → numpy.diff() on beat timestamps
      What it measures: The spacing between rhythmic pulses, normalized
      by the average tempo. This captures the "groove feel" independent
      of absolute BPM — two tracks at different tempos can have the same
      groove pattern if the ratios between their beat spacings match.
      Similarity method: Histogram comparison (cosine) of normalized IOIs.

  [5] SPECTRAL CONTRAST
      Tool: librosa.feature.spectral_contrast(y=audio, sr=sr)
      What it measures: The difference in energy between spectral peaks
      and valleys across 7 frequency sub-bands. Acts as an "EQ fingerprint"
      — captures how a producer balanced the frequency spectrum.
      Similarity method: Cosine similarity between mean vectors.

  [6] ONSET STRENGTH CROSS-CORRELATION
      Tool: librosa.onset.onset_strength() → numpy.correlate()
      What it measures: Whether the rhythmic pulse of one track aligns
      with the other at any time offset. Finds the best rhythmic alignment
      regardless of where each track starts.
      Similarity method: Maximum normalized cross-correlation coefficient.

  [7] SUB-BASS / 808 PATTERN
      Tool: librosa.stft() → frequency mask (30–120 Hz) → 16 segments
      What it measures: How the low-frequency (sub-bass) energy moves
      across the song — where the 808s are heavy and where they drop.
      Similarity method: Cosine similarity between 16-segment envelopes.

  STEP-BY-STEP PROCESS:
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Step 1: Load both WAV files at 44,100 Hz sample rate
    Step 2: Compute all 7 metrics for each track independently
    Step 3: Compute pairwise cosine similarity for each metric
    Step 4: Run HPSS stem separation to measure residual energy

  WHY 6 SEMITONES IS SIGNIFICANT:
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    A 6-semitone (tritone) shift is the maximum possible distance between
    any two notes before you start returning to the same pitch class.
    It is the furthest you can transpose from a given key while staying
    within the chromatic scale. Producers who want to obscure a sampled or
    copied beat frequently choose the tritone shift precisely because it
    makes the key sound maximally different to a casual listener.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  WHY THIS PHASE WAS NECESSARY:
  After tempo and pitch correction, Drake's track still carried 59.5%
  unclassifiable residual energy. The hypothesis was that a third layer
  of obfuscation had been applied: heavy reverb, which blurs drum transients,
  smears the beat grid, and buries rhythmic energy inside a wash of
  room reflections.

  In trap and drill production — the genres of both tracks — engineers
  keep drums dry and punchy to maintain low-end clarity. Applying enough
  reverb to hide 30% of your percussive content is not a standard
  production choice. It is anomalous.

  WHAT DEREVERBERATION DOES:
  Reverb is echo — the same sound repeating at lower volume after the
  original transient. Dereverberation algorithms identify these echoes
  and subtract them, leaving only the direct sound.

  THREE ALGORITHMS WERE APPLIED:
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  ALGORITHM 1 — SPECTRAL SUBTRACTION
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    How it works:
      1. Convert audio to STFT (Short-Time Fourier Transform) — a matrix
         showing how frequency content changes over time.
         Tool: librosa.stft(audio, n_fft=2048, hop_length=512)

      2. For each time frame, estimate how much reverb energy should be
         present based on past frames. Reverb is the sound of previous
         frames echoing forward — it decays exponentially over time.
         Formula: reverb_estimate[t] = Σ (α^k × magnitude[t - delay - k])
         where α = 0.85 (decay factor), delay = 8 frames (~92ms),
         k runs over 30 frames (~350ms reverb tail)

      3. Subtract the estimated reverb tail from each current frame:
         clean_magnitude[t] = max(magnitude[t] - reverb_estimate[t], 0)
         (floor at zero to prevent negative magnitudes)

      4. Reconstruct the waveform using the cleaned magnitudes with the
         original phase information.
         Tool: librosa.istft(clean_magnitude × exp(j × phase))

    Parameters used:
      n_fft  = 2048  (frequency resolution)
      hop    = 512   (time resolution ~12ms per frame at 44.1kHz)
      delay  = 8 frames (pre-delay before reverb starts)
      tail   = 30 frames (reverb length being estimated)
      alpha  = 0.85  (reverb decay rate)

  ALGORITHM 2 — CEPSTRAL LIFTERING
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    How it works:
      1. Take the log of the STFT magnitude spectrum.
         log_mag = log(|STFT|)

      2. Apply an inverse FFT to get the real cepstrum — a representation
         where the room's echo (reverb) lives at high "quefrency" values.
         cepstrum = IFFT(log_mag)

      3. Zero out the late quefrency components (reverb tail).
         Keep only the first n_fft/12 ≈ 170 samples (direct sound only).
         lifter = cepstrum with all values above cutoff set to zero.

      4. Transform back to log-magnitude spectrum, then reconstruct audio.
         clean = exp(FFT(lifter))

    This works because reverb occupies a specific, predictable region of
    cepstral space, separate from the direct sound.

  ALGORITHM 3 — SPECTRAL GATING
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    How it works:
      1. Compute the average energy of each STFT frame.
      2. Identify the bottom 35% of frames by energy — these are reverb
         tail frames (the echo after each sound has decayed).
      3. Apply a smooth gate mask that suppresses low-energy (reverb) frames
         while preserving high-energy (direct sound) frames.
         Tool: scipy.ndimage.uniform_filter1d for smooth transitions.

    This is the most aggressive method — it mutes frames where reverb
    dominates and preserves frames where the direct signal dominates.

  BEST RESULT: Spectral Subtraction (Algorithm 1)
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  WHY THIS PHASE WAS NECESSARY:
  Full-mix comparisons include vocals, melody, harmony, and production
  effects which all influence the scores. To get the most direct possible
  comparison — the drums themselves, nothing else — we stripped every
  other element from both tracks and compared only the percussion.

  THE HYPOTHESIS: If "Little Birdie" used Rugrat's beat as a source,
  the underlying drum pattern and drum sounds should match Rugrat's
  drums even after all the production processing is stripped away.

  STEP-BY-STEP PROCESS:
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    Step 1: Run HPSS on both tracks with aggressive margin=6.0
            (higher margin = more aggressive source separation)
            Tool: librosa.decompose.hpss(librosa.stft(audio), margin=6.0)

            HPSS works by separating the STFT matrix into:
              H (harmonic) — horizontal structures in the spectrogram
                             (sustained tones, notes, melodies)
              P (percussive) — vertical structures in the spectrogram
                              (sharp transients, drum hits)

            The margin=6.0 setting enforces stricter separation — anything
            ambiguous gets excluded from both stems rather than blended.

    Step 2: Extract percussive stems from both tracks
            Rugrat  → Rugrat_Percussive_Isolated.wav / .mp3
            Drake   → Drake_Dereverbed_Percussive_Isolated.wav / .mp3

    Step 3: Run ALL comparison metrics on the percussion stems ONLY:
            — BPM detection on drums only
            — Onset detection (exact drum hit timestamps)
            — IOI groove on drum hits only
            — MFCC on percussion stem (drum timbre)
            — Onset cross-correlation (beat grid alignment)
            — 32-step rhythm grid fingerprint
            — Spectral contrast on drums
            — Frequency band energy analysis (kick/snare/hat breakdown)

    Step 4: Compare results and compute similarity scores

  13A. WAVEFORM CLIPPING ANALYSIS
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  TOOL USED:
    numpy.where(numpy.abs(audio) >= 0.99) — identifies every sample
    that has hit or exceeded 99% of full-scale amplitude (the digital
    ceiling). Groups of consecutive clipped samples = one "clip event."

  RESULTS:
    Metric                   Rugrat "Intro"     Drake "Little Birdie"
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Peak amplitude           1.1388 (1.13 dBFS)  1.1696 (1.36 dBFS)
    RMS level               -14.39 dBFS          -11.61 dBFS
    Crest factor             15.52 dB             12.97 dB
    Samples above 0.99       163                  2,406
    Clip events              162                  1,055
    Avg clip duration        0.05 ms              0.10 ms
    Max clip duration        0.09 ms              1.86 ms
    First clip at            10.93s               0.56s
    Last  clip at            115.23s              168.94s

  WHAT CREST FACTOR MEANS:
    Crest factor = peak level minus RMS level. It measures how much
    headroom exists between the loudest moments and the average loudness.
    A high crest factor (>16 dB) = dynamic — loud peaks, quiet valleys.
    A low crest factor (<10 dB) = over-compressed — peaks and valleys
    smashed together by a limiter.

    Rugrat: 15.52 dB — normal, moderately dynamic production.
    Drake : 12.97 dB — noticeably more compressed. 2.55 dB less crest
    means Drake's limiter was pushed significantly harder.

  THE 6.5× CLIP EVENT DISPARITY — FORENSICALLY SIGNIFICANT:
    Drake has 1,055 clip events vs Rugrat's 162 — 6.5 times more.
    Drake's clips start at 0.56 seconds (nearly from the first beat).
    Drake's maximum clip duration is 1.86ms vs Rugrat's 0.09ms —
    20× longer individual clips.

    INTERPRETATION: When a producer layers additional sounds on top of
    an existing beat — additional 808 bass, new synths, vocal effects —
    the cumulative energy pushes the output level higher. To keep the
    final master under 0 dBFS, the engineer hits the limiter harder.
    Hitting a limiter hard creates more and longer clip events.

    Rugrat's track is self-produced with moderate compression.
    Drake's track shows the signature of heavy additional layering
    being crammed through an aggressive master limiter — consistent
    with a source beat having additional production stacked on top of it.
